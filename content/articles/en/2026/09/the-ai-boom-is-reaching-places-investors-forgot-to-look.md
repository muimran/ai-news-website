Data centre money is landing in towns that were promised fibre optics a decade ago and never got them. Six months of records requests across four divisions show who is actually being paid — and who is paying.

## What we found

Reporting for this investigation began with a single question and a records request that took four months to answer.[^rti] The documents that came back described a process nobody involved was willing to explain on the record, so we went to the people it affected instead.

What follows is drawn from eighteen interviews, a review of contracts obtained under freedom of information law,[^contracts] and two weeks of reporting on the ground. Where we could not confirm a claim independently, we have said so.

In short: in four towns, land was set aside, power was promised and tax was waived for buildings that will run AI for customers who are almost all abroad. The towns were told this would bring jobs and a faster internet. So far it has brought fences, a substation and a water bill that someone else is paying.

## The towns

None of the four is a place an investor would have named five years ago. Two sit on the edge of economic zones that were planned for garment and electronics factories and never filled. One is a river town whose main road floods most monsoons. The fourth is a market town an hour from the nearest city, where the fastest connection in 2016 was a mobile signal that dropped at the mosque.

What they share is cheap land, a spare connection to the national grid, and officials eager for anything with the word "technology" in it. A decade ago each was promised fibre optic cable as part of a national push to connect rural Bangladesh. In three of them, residents told us, the cable arrived at the government office and went no further.

The new arrivals are not shops or offices. They are long, windowless sheds of the kind that hold servers: a data centre in all but the name on the gate. Two are finished, two are still being built, and none of them employs more than a few dozen people from the town.

"They told us it was a technology park," said a teacher in the river town, who asked that we not use her name because her husband works on the site. "I thought my students would learn computers there. There is nothing for them inside."

## The money

On paper the buildings belong to local companies, most of them registered in the last three years. The contracts we obtained show who pays for what. The land is leased from the state at a fraction of the market rate for thirty years. The companies pay no import duty on equipment, which in a data centre means the costliest part of the bill: racks of GPUs, the chips that train and run AI models, each costing more than a family in the town earns in a decade.

Behind the local companies are customers who rent the computing power by the hour. Their names are not in the contracts. Two people who have worked on the sites told us the main users are overseas firms that need cheap compute to train models, and that the work done inside has no connection to Bangladesh at all.

That is not, in itself, wrong. Countries compete for this business, and Bangladesh has every right to want a share of it. The question the documents raise is who is carrying the cost.

> "The building is ours on paper. Everything that makes it valuable belongs to someone else."
> — An engineer who has worked on two of the sites

## The power

A data centre draws power without pause, day and night, at a level a town of this size has never had to supply. The substation built for one of the sites, the documents show, was paid for out of the regional power company's own budget, not by the company that needed it.[^substation]

The tariff the site pays is a concession rate, agreed in advance for ten years and lower than the rate charged to the town's small factories.[^tariff] When the grid is short, as it often is in summer, the agreement gives the site priority. Residents in two of the towns described power cuts that began, they said, in the same months the sites came online. We could not confirm the link from the grid's own records, which were not released to us, and the power company did not answer our questions about it.

A rice mill owner in the market town showed us his bills for the last two years. His spending on diesel for a backup generator had tripled. "When the lights go off, theirs stay on," he said. "Everyone here knows that now."

## The water

Servers run hot, and the cheapest way to cool them is with water. Of the four sites, three draw groundwater; the fourth draws from the river. The permits we obtained set no limit on how much they may take.[^water]

In the river town, the water table under the site has fallen by more than a metre in two years, according to readings taken by a local school's science club and shared with us. One reading is not a study, and other wells nearby have fallen too. But the town's tube wells are shallow, and families at the edge of the site told us theirs now run dry in March, a month earlier than before.

> "Nobody asked us. We found out the way everyone else found out."
> — A shopkeeper beside the site's main gate

## Who was asked

The law requires a public notice and a period for objections before land is leased for industry. In three of the four towns, the notice was posted, as the law allows, on the board outside the district office. The minutes of the meetings that approved the leases record no objections.[^minutes]

When we showed the minutes to people who live beside the sites, none had heard of the meetings. Two local councillors told us they learned about the projects when the fences went up. A third said he had voted in favour because he was told the sites would bring the internet to every house, and that he had not been shown the contracts.

The training data that the models inside learn from, and the products they become, will be sold elsewhere. What stays in the town is the building, the substation and the hole in the water table.

## The pattern

The same arrangement appears in each of the four places we checked. Each time, the terms were slightly different and the outcome was roughly the same: a decision made quickly, at a scale nobody had planned for, in a jurisdiction where the rules had not yet been written.

None of this is unique to Bangladesh. Towns in other countries have made the same trade, and some have done well out of it, where they negotiated hard: a share of the revenue, a cap on water, local hiring written into the lease. In the contracts we read, there is none of that. The benefits promised to the towns appear only in the speeches made when the sites were opened.

## What happens next

Two of the authorities we contacted have opened reviews. A third declined to comment. The economic zones authority replied after this story was first published, and said the leases had followed the rules in force at the time.

We will continue reporting this story, and we publish corrections in the open. If you know something about it, or live near one of these sites, the newsroom's contact details are at the bottom of this page.
